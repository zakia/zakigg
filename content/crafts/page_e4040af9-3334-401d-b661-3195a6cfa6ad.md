## 1. Build a career-history sheet

Top-grading is a structured chronological review of your education and jobs, covering decisions, accomplishments, setbacks, relationships, and performance patterns. The official methodology commonly examines every role rather than relying only on polished behavioural examples. [Topgrading overview](https://topgrading.com/hiring), [Topgrading interview questions](https://blog.topgrading.com/new-blog/resources/blog/the-one-blockbuster-hiring-question).

For every position on your résumé, write brief answers to:

* What were you hired to accomplish?
* What were your three most important results?
* What did you personally own versus what the team owned?
* What was difficult or unsuccessful?
* What feedback did you receive?
* Who was your manager, and what would they identify as your strengths and weaknesses?
* Why did you join, and why did you leave?
* What changed because of your work?

Add numbers wherever possible: revenue, adoption, latency, conversion, incidents, costs, delivery time, users, or team size. Expect follow-ups such as “Why?”, “How did you know?”, “What did you personally do?”, and “What would your manager say?”

Keep explanations of departures factual and neutral. Never blame a former manager or disguise a failure as a strength. A credible answer follows this pattern:

> I expected X, the evidence showed Y, and I was responsible for Z. I changed A, which produced B. The lesson I carried forward was C.

## 2. Prepare one flagship project

Aim for an 8–10 minute initial version that can expand under questioning:

1. **Context:** client or business problem, scale, and constraints.
2. **Goal:** the outcome and how success was measured.
3. **Your ownership:** decisions and work you personally performed.
4. **Trade-offs:** alternatives considered and why you chose this path.
5. **Execution:** how you worked across functions and handled disagreement.
6. **Result:** quantified impact.
7. **Reflection:** mistake, feedback, and what you would change now.

Select a project that demonstrates several Wealthsimple values naturally:

* **The client, the client, the client:** you uncovered or prioritized a genuine user need.
* **Ship it:** you reduced scope, launched, learned, and iterated.
* **We are maker-owners:** you found and advanced a problem without waiting for instructions.
* **Naive ambition:** you pursued a materially better outcome despite uncertainty.
* **Be human:** you handled conflict candidly and respectfully.
* **Keep it simple:** you removed product, technical, or organizational complexity.

Those are Wealthsimple’s current stated values. Its culture manual also says employees are expected to stay close to the work, communicate clearly in a fast and asynchronous environment, earn trust through action, and actively build with AI. [Wealthsimple Culture Manual](https://www.wealthsimple.com/en-ca/culture).

Do not repeatedly name the values during the interview. Show them through your decisions and let the interviewers recognize the match.

## 3. Prepare a specific “Why Wealthsimple?”

A strong answer can combine these points:

* **Mission:** helping everyone achieve financial freedom by making sophisticated financial products understandable and accessible. [About Wealthsimple](https://www.wealthsimple.com/en-ca/about)
* **Operating model:** small teams, ownership, rapid shipping, direct client impact, and limited bureaucracy. [Wealthsimple Careers](https://www.wealthsimple.com/en-ca/careers)
* **Current stage:** Wealthsimple reported $155.6 billion in assets and 3.6 million clients at the end of Q2 2026, with $17 billion in quarterly net flows. It is operating at substantial financial-services scale while still launching new categories. [Q2 2026 results](https://newsroom.wealthsimple.com/)
* **Product ambition:** its recent expansion includes IPO access, broader family and business offerings, and a regulated prediction-markets product. [Wealthsimple Newsroom](https://newsroom.wealthsimple.com/)

Example:

> Wealthsimple appeals to me because it combines consumer-product craft with the responsibility and complexity of regulated financial services. The company has reached significant scale, but its culture still expects individuals to remain close to the work, ship, and own outcomes. My experience doing \[specific relevant work] fits that environment, particularly because I’ve learned how to simplify \[relevant complexity] while protecting \[client trust, reliability, compliance, or quality].

Only use product or mission points you genuinely care about.

## 4. Rehearse the most likely questions

Practice these aloud, with answers under two minutes before follow-ups:

* Walk us through your career.
* Why did you make each transition?
* What are you most proud of?
* What was your biggest failure or lowest point?
* Tell us about difficult feedback you initially resisted.
* What would each former manager say you need to improve?
* Tell us about a disagreement with a strong colleague.
* When did you act without permission?
* When did speed matter, and what did you deliberately leave out?
* When did you advocate for the client against internal convenience?
* What complex thing did you make simpler?
* How do you use AI in your current work?
* Why Wealthsimple, this team, and this role now?

Prepare two genuine weaknesses with evidence that you have changed your behaviour. “I care too much” answers will undermine the self-awareness they are testing.

## 5. Ask questions that test mutual fit

Choose three or four:

* “What outcomes would make you say this hire had an exceptional first year?”
* “Which Wealthsimple value is hardest for this team to practise consistently?”
* “Can you describe a recent case where the team shipped early and changed direction from client feedback?”
* “Where does this role have real decision-making authority?”
* “What distinguishes people who become highly trusted here from people who struggle?”
* “What important problem is currently falling between teams?”
* “How has the expectation to build with AI changed this team’s day-to-day work?”
* “As Wealthsimple grows, where is complexity beginning to challenge the ‘keep it simple’ principle?”
